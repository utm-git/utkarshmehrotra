1. Config-Driven Multi-Geography Financial Systems — the strongest angle
The specific problem: how do you build a lending platform that operates correctly across Thailand, Indonesia, Philippines, and India simultaneously — each with different payment rails, different regulatory settlement windows, different currency behaviour — without forking the codebase per market?
The contribution is the config-driven market onboarding architecture: a single system whose behaviour is fully parameterised by market configuration rather than market-specific code paths. The claim worth making in a paper is that this pattern — which we might call configuration-as-correctness boundary — reduces onboarding time from weeks to days while preserving financial correctness guarantees across all markets.
This maps to the systems/middleware track at conferences like VLDB, OSDI, or EuroSys. It could also land at FinTech or ICSE depending on how it's framed.
What you'd need to write it: the formal definition of a market configuration schema, a proof sketch that config-driven dispatch preserves idempotency and consistency invariants across markets, and empirical data on onboarding time before and after.

2. Exactly-Once Loan Lifecycle Management Under Third-Party Rail Failures — the most technically rigorous angle
The problem: a distributed state machine for loan lifecycle management must process repayment events with exactly-once semantics when the upstream payment rail (a third-party provider in each geography) can deliver duplicate events, delayed events, and out-of-order events simultaneously.
The contribution is a formal treatment of how to achieve exactly-once loan state transitions when the input stream does not guarantee exactly-once delivery — the gap between what payment rails promise and what financial applications require. This is a cleaner version of the classic distributed systems problem (at-least-once delivery + idempotent consumers) but with the additional complexity of state machine transitions that are not naturally idempotent (a loan moving from ACTIVE to OVERDUE cannot simply be retried).
This maps to SIGMOD, VLDB, or IEEE Transactions on Dependable and Secure Computing. It's the most technically rigorous of the three and would require the most formal treatment.
What you'd need: a formal state machine definition, a proof of correctness under the failure model (duplicate delivery, out-of-order delivery, partial failure), and empirical validation showing zero duplicate state transitions across the production observation period.

3. Real-Time Delinquency Scoring as a Stream Processing Problem — the most accessible angle
The problem: traditional delinquency detection is batch — run a job nightly, flag accounts that have missed payments. The claim is that real-time delinquency scoring using repayment behaviour signals (not just missed payments but early signals: partial payments, payment timing drift, repeated retry patterns) catches at-risk accounts days earlier than batch approaches, enabling earlier intervention and measurably better collection outcomes.
The contribution is the streaming feature engineering for early delinquency signals and a demonstration that real-time scoring on behavioural signals outperforms batch scoring on binary payment outcomes.
This maps to KDD, WWW, or AAAI (applied ML track) rather than systems conferences. It's more ML-paper than systems-paper.
What you'd need: the feature set (repayment behaviour signals beyond binary miss/pay), a comparison of early detection rates between batch and streaming approaches, and outcome data showing intervention effectiveness.
